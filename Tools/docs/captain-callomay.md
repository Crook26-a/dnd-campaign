# Captain Callomay

Oct 7, 2026 · Joseph Crook

DM only. The captain of the Sallowmay, as the crew knows him and as he really is: his numbers for when the players start investigating him, what each kind of snooping turns up, and his story.

## At a glance

| **What the crew sees** | Captain Callomay, owner and captain of the Sallowmay. Lean and unhurried, eyes the green of deep water, a smile that doesn't quite reach them, a bronze-hilted sword. Courteous, dry, a little sad. A fairy named Thimble rides on his shoulder. Everyone assumes he's a warlock with a fae patron and that she's his familiar, and he lets them. |
| **What he really is** | An archfey: Calandrous Callomaydare, once called the Lord of Far Shores. The warlock is a costume. There is no patron. He is the fae. |
| **What he wants** | To pay off a wager he lost to the sea three hundred years ago, so he can stand on dry land again. |
| **What he fears** | That he'll outlive this crew too. And, quietly, that when he's finally free he'll miss the sea. |
| **Voice** | Unhurried and courteous. Calls the crew by their roles ("Navigator", "Medic") until they earn their names. Answers questions with questions when he'd rather not answer. |
| **Rules he can't break** | He never tells an outright lie. He can't set foot on land. He can't touch cold iron. He can't draw a map of a mortal coast. He keeps every bargain he makes, to the letter. |
| **The ship's rule** | No innocent can be harmed aboard the Sallowmay, and nothing innocent ends up on her table. The crew aren't covered: they're crew, not guests. See No innocents aboard. |

## The warlock act

He never claims to be a warlock. Other people say it, and he doesn't correct them. Everything he says about it is true, just carefully worded:

> "People say I made a pact with a fae. I made a bargain, yes. I've been paying for it ever since."

> "Who do I serve? The sea. She's the oldest of them all. The sailors call her Mother Brine."

> "My price? Maps. Every true chart you draw takes a little off what I owe."

Small magic he does in plain view, which looks like a warlock's tricks: a lantern lights when he touches it, ropes knot themselves, a breeze comes when he whistles, fog parts in front of the bow, and spilled water runs back into its cup. He never needs to roll for any of it.

And every warlock story has a familiar, so the crew will assume Thimble is his. She isn't (see Thimble, below). Asked about her, he tells the truth:

> "People call her my familiar. She calls herself my conscience."

> "I don't command Thimble. I ask, and most days she says yes."

## Thimble, the fairy who isn't a familiar

A sprite no bigger than a hand: dragonfly wings, a sewing needle for a sword, a thimble for a helmet. Silver markings wind around both her arms: spirals and triskelions, like old druidic knotwork. (DM only: one for each time the captain has brought her back to life. The crew doesn't know that: as far as they can tell, he heals her. See Study her silver spirals.) Bossy, nosy and fiercely loyal. She calls the captain "Callo" and the crew "the new ones." She was part of his court in the old days, helped plant the willow and the hawthorn the Sallowmay grew from, and followed him to sea when he lost the wager. Nobody ordered her to.

**What she does for him.** He can't set foot on land, so Thimble goes ashore for him: she's his eyes on the docks, his ears in the taverns, and the one who brings back gossip. She can't draw maps any more than he can; fae ink won't hold a mortal coast. When he wants to know whether someone is honest, he asks Thimble, who has looked at their heart (her Heart Sight).

**Her numbers.** Use the SRD Sprite stat block; it's on her sheet in the app. AC 15, 10 hit points, speed 10 feet and fly 40 feet. Needle Sword (+6, 1d4 + 4 piercing). Enchanting Bow (+6, 1 piercing, and the target is Charmed until the start of her next turn). Heart Sight (a creature within 5 feet makes a DC 10 Charisma save or she learns its emotions and alignment). She can turn invisible at will. She counts as crew, not a guest, so the ship's rule doesn't protect her either.

**If she falls (Joseph's table rule).** Ten hit points doesn't last long, and she's too important to lose. At 0 hit points she's dead, but not for good: if the crew brings her to the captain, he brings her back. As far as the crew can tell, he heals her: he takes her into his cabin, and she comes out a few hours later, grumbling, with one more silver spiral on her arm. Nobody says the word "dead." He tells the crew early, so they know to carry her home:

> "If you ever see Thimble fall, bring her to me anyway. She is a tough fairy, and I don't expect anything will keep her down for long."

He never has to leave the ship to do it, so it doesn't break his bargain the way the rescue does (see The rescue and the reveal). If the crew asks whether he could heal them the same way, he changes the subject.

**How she gives the game away.** A real familiar comes from the Find Familiar spell, and the spell sets rules she keeps breaking. Players who know the rules can catch it:

| **A real familiar (Find Familiar)** | **Thimble** |
| **A spirit wearing an animal's shape.** | A real fey, flesh and wings. |
| **Obeys its master's commands.** | Argues with him in front of everyone, and sometimes says no. |
| **Can't attack.** | Draws her needle sword on anyone who threatens him. |
| **At 0 hit points it disappears, dropping whatever it carried, until the spell is cast again.** | Hurt, she bleeds like anyone else. She doesn't vanish. When she falls, her body stays, and it's the captain who mends her, not a spell. |
| **Its master talks to it in their mind within 100 feet, and can see through its eyes.** | They talk out loud. She flies ashore for a whole day and reports back in person. |
| **Its master can send it away to a pocket dimension.** | When she wants to be gone, she just turns invisible. |

## When they investigate him

**He never lies, but he hides a great deal.** When they try to read him or to lie to him, it's a contest: they roll, he rolls, and the higher total wins. That's the fun of it: the kids can piece together that he's hiding something, and bit by bit what, without ever being handed it.

These numbers are tuned for the table (my call, not the rules). His Deception is +12 and his Insight +14, so his roll averages about 23 when they try to read him and about 25 when they lie to him: he is harder to fool than to read. A good roll has a real chance against him, but beating him should feel like a win. **Elves roll these two contests with advantage** (my rule): they have fey blood, and they can read one of their own. Rin and Xuna are elves.

How often they win, for a player at +4: reading him, about 1 time in 6 (an elf, rolling with advantage, about 1 in 4); lying to him, about 1 time in 9 (an elf, about 1 in 5).

### His slips

When someone beats him in the contest to catch him out, roll a d8 (or pick one that fits the moment). Each points at his secret without giving it away. Once a slip has come up, he's careful about it afterwards, so reroll repeats.

| **d8** | **The slip** |
| **1** | "The last time I walked on a beach…" He stops, and changes the subject. (He hasn't stood on land in a very long time.) |
| **2** | He speaks of Mother Brine as "the old girl," fondly and a little bitterly, the way you'd speak of someone you've met. |
| **3** | Asked his age: "Older than the harbor wall." The harbor wall is two hundred years old; Commodore Quell can confirm it. |
| **4** | He corrects a detail in a sea story no one living could know: "No, she went down on the north side. There were gulls." |
| **5** | Something iron brushes his sleeve. He pulls away before he can stop himself, and for a breath his hand has the sheen of the inside of a shell. |
| **6** | "My garden had a willow and a hawthorn." He runs his hand along the rail as he says it, as if the ship were one of them. |
| **7** | He names an island that isn't on any chart, then waves it off: "A story. Nothing more." |
| **8** | "Three hundred years," he says of a debt, then laughs it off as a figure of speech. |

**His true name.** No roll ever finds it; it comes out only in the reveal. If a player ever speaks it to him, he must answer one question with the plain truth. Unless it's used to help him, that player's favor drops to −3 for good.

## Running him in a fight

- **He doesn't join their fights.** The bargain counts only charts made by mortal choice, so he leaves the decisions, and the danger, to the crew.
- **If the ship is attacked,** the sea answers for him: a wave knocks boarders off the rail, the rigging tangles around an attacker, a sudden fog hides the ship. Use it to tip a fight, never to win it outright.
- **If someone attacks him,** blades pass through him like mist and spells unravel on his skin. He doesn't fight back. He's disappointed, which is worse. Favor drops to the bottom of the scale, −15, for whoever did it.
- **He has no hit points.** Nothing at their level can hurt him.

## No innocents aboard

**No innocent creature can be harmed on the Sallowmay.** It's the oldest law of his garden, the guest-law, older than the wager, and the ship carries it with her. Whether something is innocent is always your call.

### Fishing

When someone pulls up a fish (or a crab, or anything else), roll a die to see whether it was a good one or a bad one. A d6 works: **1 to 3, a bad fish; 4 to 6, a good fish.** Change the odds to suit the moment.

- **A bad fish** (a bully of the reef, one that ate its neighbors' eggs) can die, and it can be cooked and eaten.
- **A good fish** won't die and can't be killed, and it shows no sign of pain. The hook slides out without a mark, the knife won't cut, and it flops happily until someone throws it back.

### Food from shore

Meat bought for the ship's account comes only from animals that deserved it. The butcher doesn't know why he picks the ones he does, but he always has a story ready:

> "This pig? Mean as a snake. Bit my boy twice and chased the priest down the lane. Your captain's welcome to him."

### People

The same goes for people. Rescued sailors, a stowaway, a prisoner who has surrendered: no one can hurt them aboard. A blow meant for an innocent turns aside, a rope lets go, a deck plank tilts. Someone attacking the ship isn't innocent, so boarders can be fought as normal.

### Not the crew

**The law protects guests, and the crew aren't guests.** Passengers, rescued sailors, a stowaway, a fish pulled aboard: all guests. When the players took his hire, they signed on as crew, and crew are part of the ship. The ship takes her knocks, and so do they. If anyone asks:

> "You're not my guests. You're my crew. That's a different promise."

If a character ever quits the crew, the protection covers them again, because they're a guest once more.

### As a clue

This is one of the best clues the crew can find on their own: a fish that won't die, a knife that won't cut. If they ask the captain, he answers truthfully: "Nothing that doesn't deserve it comes to harm on my ship." Arcana DC 15 recognizes it as fae guest-law.

## His story

### The Lord of Far Shores

Calandrous Callomaydare was an archfey of the twilight courts, and he loved stories of the mortal world more than anything. He collected them the way other fae collect jewels: every coast, every harbor, every island anyone had ever sung about. He never traveled himself. He didn't need to. He boasted that no shore in the world was hidden from him.

### The wager

Three hundred years ago, at a midsummer revel by the sea, someone old and grey and patient was listening: Mother Brine, the oldest power of the sea, neither cruel nor kind. She offered him a wager. If he could name every island in her sea, she'd give him any one thing he asked. If he couldn't, he would owe her.

He named a thousand islands. She named one he'd never heard of: an island she keeps hidden, that no one has ever charted. He lost.

### The price

- He must sail her sea until **every coast in it has been charted by mortal hands** and given to mortal people.
- Until then, **he can't set foot on land.**
- **He can't draw the maps himself.** Fae ink won't hold a mortal coast; by morning the lines have wandered off the page. So he needs a crew.
- Only **charts made by mortal choice** count. That's why he lets the crew make every decision.

### The Sallowmay

The ship is the last piece of home he brought with him. She was grown, not built, from a willow and a hawthorn in his own garden. Sallow is an old word for willow, and may is the hawthorn's old name. That's why she never needs repairs: she heals. He loves her the way you'd love a dog that has followed you across the world.

### Three hundred years of crews

He has had many crews. Some grew old and went home. Some died, and he couldn't save them without breaking the rule. Some broke their word to him. He has learned to keep a little distance, and to let the crew make the choices, partly because the bargain demands it and partly because it hurts less.

He's quietly delighted by the Guild of Explorers: a whole guild of mortals paid to chart coasts. Every map they file counts toward his debt. It's why he sends his crews to them.

### The hidden island

Here's the catch he doesn't talk about: until the island Mother Brine named is charted, the debt can never be paid. He doesn't know where it is. Your campaign notes have "a place that isn't on any map" as an island idea. That's this island, and it's the campaign's ending: the crew finds it, charts it, and sets him free.

**It's hidden under stars (Joseph, 2026-10-09).** The night she won the wager, Mother Brine drowned a handful of stars in her sea. The island lies where they point. No one can see those stars any more, so no one can steer by them, and you can't see the island even from a mile off. You can only reach it by sailing the course they set.

- **The key is the Stargazer's spectacles.** They show the wearer the stars for 10 minutes a day, from anywhere, and that includes the drowned ones. Whoever wears them sees a small constellation of pale stars that's on no chart. Let the players name it.
- **Xuna is the one to wear them.** She's the navigator, and a Circle of the Stars druid, so her magic and the spectacles fit together. Ten minutes of stars a day gives one bearing a day, so the voyage is slow: a few days of sailing, a look through the spectacles, a correction to the course.
- **Lady Moreau has them (Joseph, 2026-10-09),** and she thinks they're broken: "Put them on and the room goes dark and fills with little lights. Ten minutes, then nothing until tomorrow. Charming. Useless." She bought them at a salvage auction, off a wreck no one could name. They sit in a glass case in her gallery, in plain view. She'd trade them for anything that glows.
- **What he knows.** He doesn't know about the drowned stars, and he's never heard of the spectacles. If they tell him, he goes very still. Slip 7 (he names an island no chart shows) is him guessing at it. He's guessed wrong for three hundred years.
- **Vesperine wants the spectacles lost** (see The rival's patron, below). If Rupert hears they exist, they become his errand.

### Signposting the spectacles

You don't know how many sessions the campaign will run, so tie the clues to **what happens**, not to session numbers. Plant the spectacles early, drop one clue each time the crew comes home, and pull the turn whenever you're ready to start the ending. A short campaign skips most of the clues; a long one spaces them out. Either way, the spectacles are already sitting where the players have seen them.

**1. Plant them early (the first time the crew visits Moreau Villa).** Lady Moreau offers 50 gold for the lantern, so the crew may meet her as early as Session 1. Put the spectacles in the case next to where the lantern would go. If anyone asks, she gives her "broken" speech and moves on to something that glows. That's all: no roll, no music cue. Let them forget about them.

**2. One clue per homecoming.** When the crew comes back to Kalatecha after an island, drop one of these, in any order. Skip any that don't fit, and stop when you've used enough.

| **Clue** | **Where it comes from** |
| "There used to be more stars over this sea. Ask anyone old enough." | Thimble, on a clear night at sea, out of nowhere. |
| An old sea-song: "Seven stars the Brine put out, and seven stars to steer by." Greta says her gran sang it, and nobody knows what it means. | Greta Vasquez, at her net loft. |
| The captain names an island that's on no chart, then says it's a story (slip 7). | An Insight win against him. |
| Fontaine shows them an old Guild chart with a blank patch every navigator avoids. Nothing has ever been found there, and no one can say why they steer around it. | The Charter House, when the crew sells a map. |
| Lady Moreau complains, at one of her parties, that the "broken" spectacles show the wrong sky: "Not one star I know." | Moreau Villa, if they bring her something that glows. |
| Xuna dreams of stars underwater. Story only: no roll and no rules effect. | Xuna, after a long rest aboard. |
| Nib is seen at Moreau Villa, or Rupert asks the Guild about Lady Moreau's collection. | Anywhere in the city. |

**3. The turn (when you want the ending to start, about two or three islands out).** Vesperine learns what the spectacles are, and Rupert goes after them. He tries to buy them first. If Lady Moreau won't sell, he sends Nib to steal them. Now the crew has to get them first, or get them back. This works the same whether it comes at session 6 or session 26.

- **If you're short on time:** let the crew trade Lady Moreau something that glows (the lantern, or the firebird feather if they freed it) before Rupert gets there. One scene.
- **If you have time:** Rupert gets them first, and getting them back is its own adventure: the Undaunted, Hargrove House, or wherever he's hidden them.

**4. The first look.** Someone puts them on and sees a small constellation of pale stars that's on no chart. Let the players name it. If it's Xuna, describe it as a constellation she feels she should have known all along. Then they show the captain, and he goes very still.

**5. The voyage.** One bearing a day, ten minutes at a time. That's the campaign's last voyage.

### What freedom would mean

He wants to walk on a beach again, feel sand, sit under a tree that isn't a ship. He's afraid he'll miss the sea, and the crew, and he'd never say so.

## The rescue and the reveal

Your notes set this up: if a character dies, he appears beside them out of thin air and brings them back. It's the only time he ever leaves the ship. Now it has a cost that ties into the backstory: **stepping off the Sallowmay breaks the terms of the wager, and Mother Brine takes her due.**

- **His price:** the glamour breaks for good. From then on the crew sees him as he really is. And Mother Brine hides one more island somewhere in her sea, so his debt just got longer (and the crew has a new place to find). He doesn't regret it.
- **The character's price:** as in your notes, a small fae mark, such as a silver mark on the skin or a favor owed. A good one: the revived character can now hear the sea talking, faintly, at night.

Read when he appears:

> The air beside you folds like a sail catching wind, and the captain is there, kneeling on the stones, which he has never once done. Salt water runs from his boots. Where his face was, there's something older: skin with the sheen of the inside of a shell, hair drifting as if underwater, eyes like the deep sea with light far down in it. He lays a hand on your friend's chest and speaks a word you feel in your teeth. Far away, the tide turns.

Then let him say his name, the whole of it, once.

## Hooks in play

Picked by Joseph, 2026-10-09.

### The rival's patron: Vesperine of the Thorn

Rupert Hargrove-Vance really is a warlock, and his patron is Vesperine of the Thorn, an Unseelie archfey of the twilight courts and the captain's rival for centuries. She collected mortal songs the way he collected mortal shores, and they tried to outdo each other at every revel. When he boasted that no shore was hidden from him, she was there. When he lost, she laughed loudest.

- **What she wants:** for him to stay lost: bound to the sea forever, and his place at court hers. So the crew must never finish a chart. She pays Rupert in luck to see to it.
- **How she found Rupert:** fifteen years ago, right after he broke his word to the captain (see Rupert and Fontaine's past). A man with a grudge against the Sallowmay was exactly the tool she needed.
- **Thimble knew her at court.** She won't say the name out loud. She calls her "the Thorn Lady," or just "her," and spits over the rail afterwards.
- **An open question:** did Vesperine invite Mother Brine to that midsummer revel? The captain has wondered for three hundred years. Whether it's true is your call.

### Rupert and Fontaine's past

Fifteen years ago, Rupert and a teenage Michael Fontaine crewed the Sallowmay together. That's the voyage that "went wrong": Rupert broke his word to the captain by selling a chart to someone other than the Guild. The ship's luck turned on him, and Vesperine found him soon after.

- It explains his grudge against the crew: they have the ship and the captain he lost.
- It explains why Fontaine trusts the captain without understanding him, and why he sends the crew to him.
- **The silver swirl on his neck** (Joseph, 2026-10-10). Before the voyage went wrong, the captain saved Rupert's life aboard the Sallowmay, the same way he brings Thimble back, and it left a swirl of silver on the side of Rupert's neck, just peeking out above his collar. Rupert knows the captain saved him, and never mentions it. A player who studies it can read it the way they'd read Thimble's spirals (Intelligence (Arcana), see Study her silver spirals): fae magic, the mark of being called back from very far away. A sharp player who has seen both may put Rupert's swirl and Thimble's spirals together.
- Fontaine won't talk about it at first. Pushed, he says: "Rupert made a promise to the captain and broke it. I've never seen luck turn on a man so fast."

### Two sprites

Rupert's familiar, Nib, from Pact of the Chain, looks like a sprite: it's a spirit wearing a sprite's shape, at a warlock's beck and call. It rides invisible on his collar. Its sheet is in the Library. Thimble is the real thing, and she can tell at a glance. It makes her furious. If the crew ever sees both together, a sharp player might notice the difference: one argues, one obeys.

### The firebird

It's caged in cold iron on Lantern Rock, the one thing the captain can't touch. He can't go there, and he won't ask, but he'll be very glad if they free it. The feather glows brighter when he's near.

### Small clues around the city

- **Greta's gran.** Ask Greta Vasquez about the captain and she says: "Green eyes? My gran used to talk about a captain with green eyes."
- **Sea-silver.** No one ever sees him pay for anything. Merchants who sell to the Sallowmay find old coins in their tills, salt-crusted, from wrecks no one remembers. Dario Benedetti has a jar of them.
- **The cathedral bells** (already in the city). Pip swears they rang by themselves the morning the crew notice went up, and they ring again when the ship leaves. It's not the captain's doing: it's Mother Brine noticing a new crew.
